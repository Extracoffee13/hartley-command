1. **Google's Agent2Agent protocol moves under the Agentic AI Foundation — MCP and A2A now sit in the same house** ([axios.com](https://www.axios.com/2026/08/17/a2a-agentic-ai-foundation-open-ai-standards)) — Announced Aug 17. A2A — the open standard by which agents publish structured 'agent cards' describing their capabilities and endpoints, then discover each other and delegate work across framework and vendor boundaries — becomes a hosted project of the Agentic AI Foundation, moving out of the Linux Foundation's broader portfolio and in alongside the Model Context Protocol. Google announced A2A on Apr 9 2025 and transferred the protocol, spec and SDKs to the Linux Foundation on Jun 23 2025, with more than 100 companies announcing support at adoption. A2A v1.0, the first stable spec, shipped **Apr 9 2026** — one year to the day after announcement — with signed agent cards, multi-protocol bindings (JSON-RPC and gRPC), version negotiation from v0.3, and multi-tenancy; the Linux Foundation put v1.0 at 150+ production organizations, 22,000+ GitHub stars and SDKs in five languages. AAIF itself has grown from fewer than 40 members at its December 2025 launch to more than 250, with Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Anthropic, OpenAI, Bloomberg, Shopify and Block among the backers. **Applicable: YES** — This is the structural item of the day and it completes a picture this report has been assembling in pieces since late July. The AAIF entry on Jul 30 logged the foundation; the Jul 20 entry logged A2A as 'a protocol dimension we don't yet use and should watch as the installed-skills registry grows.' Those are now one thing. The practical read for The Construct: the two halves of an agent fleet's plumbing — MCP for how an agent reaches a tool, A2A for how an agent reaches another agent — are governed by a single body, which means one deprecation calendar, one security posture, one set of conventions to track rather than two diverging ones. That is good news for a fleet our size. The concrete action is narrow and worth doing: The Construct already runs multi-agent handoffs by hand (Cowork as maestro, Hermes as credentialed executor, Mallard and Pulse as scheduled workers), and every one of those handoffs is bespoke. Signed agent cards are the standardized version of exactly that — cryptographic identity, declared capability, declared endpoint, verifiable across an organizational boundary without a pre-existing integration agreement. Reviewing A2A v1.0 and deciding which Construct agents should publish cards first is the same architectural question as the skills registry, arriving from the other direction. Do not migrate; do map. *(Corrected on the 10:00 ET fire: the 08:38 report dated v1.0 to March 2026 and named a founding-organization list not carried by either primary source. Actual date is Apr 9 2026.)*

2. **arXiv: 'Agent Skills Can Be Harmful' finds 307 skill-induced failures — and the damage comes from RELEVANT skills, not irrelevant ones** ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.11888v1)) — An empirical study (arXiv:2608.11888, Dong, Gao, Li, Xu, Hua and Yang) of what happens when you attach reusable skills to an LLM agent, using differential testing: paired target/reference executions where a no-skill or semantically-matched-skill run acts as a pseudo-oracle, so a failure is attributed to a skill only when the same task is otherwise solvable, or solvable more cheaply, under paired comparison. The count is 307 skill-induced failures — 125 functional failures and 182 efficiency regressions. The headline finding is counter-intuitive: functional failures are rarely caused by obviously irrelevant skills. Seemingly RELEVANT skills are the culprits, pushing the agent to incorrectly implement or outright omit task-required elements. The largest sources of the efficiency regressions are 'excessive verification' and 'heavy implementation pipelines' — skills that turn validation checklists and construction recipes into mandatory work. The paper ships SkillTriage, a taxonomy-guided attribution tool that normalizes paired cases, extracts differential evidence and produces triage reports. It sits in a live cluster with 'More Skills, Worse Agents? Skill Shadowing' (2605.24050), 'The Regression Tax' (2607.22520) and 'Field Aware Agent Skill Retrieval' (2608.02880). **Applicable: YES** — This lands directly on The Construct's largest and least-measured asset. We run roughly ninety installed skills and a daily/weekly scan whose whole purpose is to ADD more, on the standing assumption that more skills means more capability. This paper says that assumption is wrong in a specific and expensive way, and names the exact failure mode our own skill library is built to produce. Two of our recurring patterns are named as the top efficiency regressions: 'excessive verification' is precisely what review-build-before-claiming-done, verify-before-claiming and verify-screen-before-claiming-unavailable enforce, and 'heavy implementation pipelines' is precisely what the b9-answer-page-factory and the ANS build skills encode. Those skills exist for good reasons — they were written to stop real, repeated mistakes — but the paper's point is that the cost is real too and we have never measured it. The finding that RELEVANT skills do the functional damage is what makes this urgent rather than interesting: it means the skills most likely to fire on a given task are the ones most likely to distort it, so triggering accuracy is not a safety property. Concrete action: adopt the paired-execution method as our own eval. Take one representative task per skill family, run it with the skill and without, and compare outcome and token cost. That converts the installed-skills registry from a list of things we have into a ledger of things that pay for themselves, and it is the missing measurement half of the skills-scan pillar. Read alongside bullet 3 and arXiv 2604.08224, still on the reading list from Aug 18.

3. **arXiv: 138,133 public SKILL.md files audited — 91.8% carry a defect, over half cannot route, and a THREE-rule linter catches most of it** ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.08453v1)) — 'What Keeps Agent Skills from Being Reusable? Evidence from 138K SKILL.md Files' (arXiv:2608.08453v1, submitted Aug 9 2026; Zhang, Liu, Chen and Ji, CUNY / Ohio State). The authors deduplicated 138,133 public SKILL.md files from 20,556 repositories and ran a two-tier, 31-check defect taxonomy grounded in the official spec. Findings: 91.8% of skills carry at least one detected defect (robust across thresholds at 88.8–94.6%), averaging 2.5 defects each. The top three are all mundane packaging problems, not attacks — missing trigger guidance in the description (52.3%), name repeated as an H1 heading (44.3%) and too many inline examples (32.1%). 67.0% have at least one routing defect, meaning over half of all published skills have descriptions that cannot reliably guide an agent to select them; a BM25 stress test over 20,000 skills confirms routing-clean skills are retrieved more reliably (88.5% vs 82.6% Hit@1). Defect density rises sharply with size — skills over 500 lines average 4.76 defects against 1.48 for skills under 50 lines — and spec-aware authoring is the single strongest quality correlate (1.83 vs 3.00 defects, Cliff's δ = −0.40). AI-generated-marked skills average 38% more defects, with safety defects 2.3× and portability defects 2.8× more common. The authors' explanatory hypothesis is 'conversation-first provenance': most public skills are chat transcripts serialized to disk, which explains why weak routing metadata, install instructions, hardcoded paths and model names co-occur. The payoff is a documented ROI elbow at three guidelines — G1 (write a ≥30-character description in '[Verb] [what]. Use when [trigger]' form), G4 (do not repeat the skill name as an H1) and G7 (externalize code and examples out of the body) — which together cover 71.9% of observed defect instances while flagging 85.9% of skills. Automated LLM repair fixed 58.4% of detected defects introducing only 0.06 new ones, but had a **0% fix rate on safety-bypass and prompt-injection defects**, so safety must be a non-bypassable gate rather than a repair pass. **Applicable: YES** — This is the operational half that bullet 2 is missing, and it arrived in the same 24 hours. Bullet 2 tells us skills silently cost us; this paper tells us exactly which defects to look for and gives a three-rule linter cheap enough to run against our whole library today. The two findings that hit The Construct hardest are size and routing. Our skills are large — the ANS, HyperCube and b9-answer-page-factory families are exactly the multi-hundred-line procedural documents this paper puts in the 4.76-defects-average band — and our descriptions are the thing we have tuned most for triggering, which is the one dimension the paper measures functionally. G1 is already close to our house style ('Use when… / Trigger on…'), which is a real result: it means our routing metadata is probably above the 52.3% baseline and we should verify rather than assume. G4 and G7 are almost certainly violated across the library. Concrete action, and it is a morning's work: run the three checks over ~/.claude/skills, sort by body length, and fix G7 first on the largest skills — externalizing code blocks and examples out of SKILL.md bodies directly reduces the token load that bullet 2 measures as efficiency regression. The 0%-repair finding on safety defects is the one to record permanently: our credentialed skills (hermes-agent-control, b9-site-deploy, b9-verify-and-index, ans-engine-autonomy) must be reviewed by hand, never by an automated repair loop.

The obvious signal is commoditization: agent hosting is now a checkbox next to web hosting, priced and supported like a LAMP stack, which compresses the part of PRAGMA's pitch that rests on 'we can stand this up and you can't.' The infrastructure lift is no longer the moat; the operating model, the client-specific skills and the attribution loop are. The re-verification makes the gap precise, and it is the whole PRAGMA argument in one sentence: Cloudways validates the RUNTIME and isolates the CONTAINER, and neither of those touches the SKILLS the operator installs into the agent. So the managed-hosting layer has professionalized everything except the layer that actually decides what the agent does. That is the shape of a real PRAGMA consulting surface, and it is closer to what we already sell than the policy items of the last two days: not 'we will build you an agent,' but 'you have deployed agents running unaudited public skills on managed infrastructure that does not audit them, and here is the harness.'
